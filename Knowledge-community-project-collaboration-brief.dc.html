A practical community project collaboration brief

Use a community project collaboration brief to clarify purpose, people, contributions, limits and review points before beginning shared work together.

Give a shared idea a clear shape

A community project collaboration brief is a short shared account of why a piece of work matters, what people will do, what support it needs and how everyone will know whether to continue. It helps turn a promising conversation into an understandable proposal. This guide is for community teams and individual contributors preparing a small collaboration, including people exploring work with Rainbow Sanctuary.

In brief: define the purpose, name responsibilities, make resource limits visible and agree how to review the work. A brief is not proof of agreement or a substitute for specialist advice where legal, financial, safeguarding or technical responsibilities require it.

Start with a specific purpose

Write the change you hope to make in ordinary language. “Create a shared orientation for new contributors” is easier to work with than “Transform the whole community system.” The broader vision can explain why you care, but the proposed work should be small enough that everyone understands what doing it would involve. State who is expected to benefit and how their perspective will be included.

Distinguish the situation you have observed from your interpretation of it. For example, “New volunteers ask similar practical questions” is an observation; “Nobody feels welcome” is a much larger conclusion. A useful brief keeps both visible so that people can question the explanation without dismissing the underlying need. This is one way to practise shared responsibility before work starts.

Describe the work and what is outside it

List a small set of intended outputs or activities. Then say what the proposal does not include. An orientation guide might include an introduction and a contact map, but not a new governance policy. A learning exchange might include one discussion and a written reflection, but not certification or ongoing mentoring. Clear exclusions make it easier to accept a bounded contribution without accepting everything around it.

Avoid describing an activity as confirmed when it is still a suggestion. Use labels such as “proposed,” “agreed” and “completed” consistently. If a participant’s name, a venue or an organization appears in a draft, check whether that person or organization has actually consented to the role being described. An attractive proposal should not announce commitments on someone else’s behalf.

Put people and decisions in the brief

Name the person responsible for each part of the work, the person who can approve changes, and the people who need to be consulted. These may be different roles. Someone who contributes specialist knowledge does not automatically become responsible for the whole project. Someone coordinating communication does not automatically have permission to make a spending or safeguarding decision.

Also agree how questions will be raised when people disagree or cannot meet a commitment. A respectful process needs room for revised capacity, not just enthusiasm at the beginning. The article on shared leadership offers a complementary perspective: distributed contribution still needs visible decisions and accountability.

Make resources and constraints explicit

Describe the time, materials, access, coordination and money the work would require. If something is unknown, mark it as an open question rather than assigning it a convenient estimate. People can then decide whether they have the capacity to proceed, whether the scope should shrink, or whether another person needs to be involved before any start date is agreed.

Clarify whether contributions are paid, voluntary or part of an exchange, and what each side understands that to mean. Do not assume that food, accommodation, land access, equipment or future opportunities are included. Those arrangements need their own explicit agreement. This template is an organizational aid, not a contract or a recommendation about the legal form of an exchange.

A reusable collaboration brief

Copy these headings into a shared document and answer them together:

  • Purpose: What are we trying to improve, and for whom?
  • Current position: What is already happening? What is still uncertain?
  • Proposed work: What will we do, and what will we deliberately leave out?
  • People: Who coordinates, contributes, decides and needs to be consulted?
  • Resources: What time, materials, access and other support are needed?
  • Boundaries: What limits, consent requirements or specialist checks apply?
  • Review: When will we compare the work with its purpose?
  • Next decision: What would lead us to continue, adjust, pause or stop?

Keep the brief readable enough that a new participant can understand it without a long explanation. Add supporting material only where it helps a decision. A document can be detailed and still fail to clarify the one question that matters, so read it once from the perspective of the person being asked to contribute.

Agree how to learn from the work

Choose a review point before starting rather than waiting for frustration or uncertainty to build. Ask what happened, what people actually experienced and what needs to change. Do not treat a positive intention as proof of a positive result. Likewise, a difficulty can be useful information rather than a reason to blame the person who noticed it.

Record the decision after review and make it available to the people involved. If the work ends, clarify who holds any outputs, what remains unfinished and what can be shared publicly. A responsible ending is part of collaboration. It keeps a small experiment from becoming an indefinite obligation and helps the next project begin with better information.

Frequently asked questions

Is a brief the same as an agreement?

Not necessarily. A brief can be a proposal, a record of discussion or a summary of agreed work. Label its status clearly. Where a collaboration creates legal, financial, safeguarding or other specialist responsibilities, the appropriate people must establish the necessary arrangements; this organizational template does not replace that work.

How detailed should the first version be?

Include enough detail to make the next decision, not every detail a future project might need. Purpose, scope, responsibilities, resources and open questions should be understandable. If a missing detail could change someone’s ability or willingness to participate, it belongs in the conversation before the work is treated as confirmed.

Can a collaboration start with a small pilot?

Yes, a bounded trial can be a useful proposal when its purpose, limits and review are clear. It should not be described as proof that a larger partnership exists. Decide what the trial can reasonably teach you and what would need a separate agreement before expanding it.

From intention to a manageable next step

A community project collaboration brief is most useful when the people involved can recognize their own understanding in it. Begin with a conversation, draft together, then check what remains unresolved. If you want to explore a contribution with Rainbow Sanctuary, use the co-creation invitation and bring the smallest version of the work that could genuinely help.
