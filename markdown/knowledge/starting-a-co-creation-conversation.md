# How to start a co-creation conversation

Start a co-creation conversation with a clear introduction: what is already alive, what you can offer, what you need, and one useful next step together.

Rainbow Sanctuary Editorial Team, Website editorial team

**Reviewed by**

**Updated**

## Begin with something real

A co-creation conversation starts with a clear account of what is already happening, what each person can contribute, and what would make a next step useful to both sides. It does not need a polished pitch or a promise to join a community. This guide is for individuals, families and project teams who want to introduce themselves to Rainbow Sanctuary without making the conversation bigger than it needs to be.

**Key takeaways:** share your actual stage, include both offers and needs, and suggest one manageable question. Curiosity is enough to begin. Availability, agreement, payment or a place in a project should never be assumed from a friendly first exchange.

## Describe the whole picture briefly

The public invitation is to bring a community, family, piece of land, practice, skill or meaningful question. Start by saying which of those best describes your situation. Then explain what is alive now. “We run a small learning group” conveys something different from “We are exploring whether to start a learning group,” and both can be useful introductions.

Keep the opening focused enough that another person can respond thoughtfully. You might describe your location, the people involved, the purpose of your work and the decision you are currently facing. There is no need to send a complete personal history, private family information or other people’s contact details. Share public links or material you have permission to share.

## Put needs beside gifts

An introduction becomes more honest when it includes the support required to make a contribution possible. Someone may offer facilitation but need a co-host and a clearly defined audience. A builder may offer practical experience but need confirmed materials and local guidance. A parent may bring useful feedback while needing flexible participation and clear safeguarding information.

Naming a need does not make it a demand. It gives both sides a way to see whether there is a real fit. The [co-creation principles](https://rainbowsanctuary.life/knowledge/co-creation-principles-for-rainbow-sanctuary) emphasize responsibility and grounded care alongside shared intention. A conversation should make it easier to say “not now” or “a smaller version would work,” not make either party feel obliged to agree.

## Separate interest from commitment

There are several different steps between an introduction and work beginning. First, people understand one another. Then they explore whether their purposes and capacities meet. Only after that can they define an activity, agree responsibilities and decide whether to proceed. Skipping those distinctions can turn an open conversation into an expectation nobody meant to create.

If an idea involves travel, a paid service, children, land access or technology handling personal information, name that early. The relevant details need their own appropriate discussion. A general co-creation exchange is not a substitute for programme information, local permission, safeguarding arrangements, a service booking or a data agreement.

## Choose a useful first question

A good opening question creates a decision the other person can actually help with. Compare “Can we transform education together?” with “Who should we speak to about offering a short parent-feedback session?” The larger aspiration can stay in the background, while the smaller question makes it possible to learn whether there is a workable relationship.

Try to avoid asking for every kind of support at once. Decide whether you first need orientation, a connection, feedback on an idea, or an answer about an existing activity. This also protects your own attention: a focused conversation can lead to a clear next step, whereas a very broad discussion may leave everyone inspired but uncertain about what happens next.

## A message starter you can adapt

Use the following as a practical outline, not a required application format:

> Hello Rainbow Sanctuary. I am writing as an individual / family / project team based in … Our work or current question is … What is already happening is … We could contribute … To do that responsibly, we would need … I would value a conversation about … Would you let us know the most appropriate person or next step?

Before sending, check that the message distinguishes a possibility from an offer you are ready to deliver. If you mention another group, say whether you are speaking for it or simply describing your own interest. If something is private, summarize the question instead of including sensitive material. Your first message should open a relationship, not disclose everything you know.

## Make the follow-up concrete

After a conversation, write a short shared summary. Note what you understood, what remains open, who will take the next action and when you will check back. A summary is especially useful across cultures and working styles: familiar words such as “partner,” “support” or “community” can mean different things to different people.

If there is no immediate fit, an honest conclusion is still valuable. You may leave with a better question, a relevant public resource or clarity that neither side has capacity now. Co-creation is not measured by how quickly everyone agrees. It is better understood through whether the relationship supports truthful decisions and responsible action.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need an established project to get in touch?

No. The homepage invitation includes questions, families, practices and individual skills as well as established projects. Say clearly whether your idea is exploratory or already operating. This lets the team understand what kind of conversation you need without mistaking an early possibility for a ready programme or partnership.

### What should I leave out of the first message?

Avoid unnecessary medical information, children’s personal details, identity documents, private contact lists and material belonging to someone else. Give enough context to identify the question, then ask how any further information should be shared. Public links and a short summary are usually more useful than an unstructured archive.

### What if I am unsure what I can contribute?

Start with your question, interests and realistic capacity. You can describe experience without promising to use it immediately. A first conversation may help identify a suitable role, or show that it is better to begin with learning and observation. There is no need to invent an offer to justify contact.

## An invitation, not an obligation

A co-creation conversation works best when both sides can remain curious and free to choose.
