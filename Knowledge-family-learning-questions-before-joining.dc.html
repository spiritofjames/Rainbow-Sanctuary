Family learning: questions before joining

Use these family learning questions to explore format, guardian involvement, children's choices, privacy and practical expectations before joining an activity.

Choose an activity through clear information

Before joining a family learning activity, ask what children will do, who holds responsibility, how guardians participate, what choices children have and how concerns will be handled. This guide is for parents and guardians exploring Rainbow Sanctuary’s current activities or the future School of the Universe. It supports an informed conversation; it does not assess a child’s development or recommend treatment.

In brief: understand the particular activity, keep the child’s voice present, and confirm practical arrangements before enrolling. Existing family activities and the School platform preparing for beta are related parts of the vision, not identical offers. Adult retreat participation is separate and voluntary, never a prerequisite for family learning.

Start with the published pathway

Read the current children and family information rather than relying only on the broad School vision. Ask which activity you are discussing, whether it is open, what its purpose is and how progression works. A title such as foundation, level or practice can have a particular meaning within a programme. Have the team explain that meaning in everyday language.

Confirm the intended ages, format, duration and guardian expectations for the actual invitation. If information appears inconsistent or is not yet confirmed, ask before making arrangements. It is reasonable to want a clear account of what participation looks like in an ordinary session, not only the aspirations that sit behind it.

Ask how children can choose

Guardian consent and a child’s willingness are related but different. Ask how facilitators notice hesitation, how a child can decline an exercise, and what happens if they want to pause or leave. The answer should make sense for the child’s age and the format of the activity. A child should not need to perform enthusiasm or share private feelings to show that they belong.

You can also ask how questions and disagreement are welcomed. Children may understand symbolic language literally or interpret an experience differently from adults. Make space for them to describe what they think without being steered toward a preferred spiritual explanation. Learning can include curiosity and uncertainty, rather than an expectation to report a special result.

Understand guardian involvement

Ask where a parent or guardian is expected to be and how they will communicate with the team. Online and in-person formats create different practical needs. Clarify supervision, joining instructions and what to do if a connection drops or a child becomes uncomfortable. Avoid assuming the same arrangement applies across every age group or programme.

It may help to discuss how you will listen afterwards. An open question such as “What was that like for you?” leaves more room than “Did you feel the energy?” The aim is not to test the child or validate an adult’s interpretation. It is to understand their experience and decide together what support or next conversation is appropriate.

Keep privacy and boundaries visible

Read the children safeguarding information and ask how it applies to the particular activity. Clarify who can contact a child, how guardians are included, whether anything is recorded or photographed, and how permission is obtained. Do not treat general participation consent as permission for every future use of a child’s image or words.

Before sending information, ask what is necessary and how it should be provided. An introductory message rarely needs a detailed family history or private health material. If an activity requires additional information, the team should explain the purpose and appropriate route. You can review the published privacy policy and raise questions before deciding what to share.

Questions you can bring to a parent conversation

Use this list to focus the discussion:

  • What is the purpose of this specific activity, and what happens during it?
  • Is it currently available, proposed or part of a beta process?
  • Who facilitates it, and what responsibility does the guardian retain?
  • How can a child ask questions, decline, pause or stop?
  • How are differing beliefs and interpretations handled?
  • What information, technology or materials are necessary?
  • Are photos, recordings or private messages involved, and how is consent managed?
  • What costs, cancellation arrangements or progression requirements apply?
  • Who should we contact with a concern before, during or after participation?

You do not need to resolve every possible future question in the first exchange. Focus on the information that affects the decision in front of you. If an answer remains unclear, note it and return to it rather than feeling obliged to enrol while uncertain.

Distinguish educational language from health promises

Rainbow Sanctuary uses spiritual and developmental language in describing its work. Ask what a term means within the teaching framework and what, if anything, is being claimed beyond that framework. A meaningful educational or spiritual experience is not the same as a proven clinical intervention. A programme should not be treated as a replacement for qualified medical, developmental or mental-health care.

This distinction also protects the child’s freedom to have an ordinary experience. A session need not produce an extraordinary perception or a dramatic change to be worth discussing. Consider whether the activity is understandable, respectful and appropriate for your family, rather than measuring participation against an advertised ideal of awakening or exceptional ability.

Frequently asked questions

Must parents join adult retreats for children to participate?

No. Adult retreat work is a separate voluntary pathway, not a condition of family or School participation. Ask about the requirements of the particular child or family activity you are considering. An interest in learning does not create an obligation to attend ceremonies, book private sessions or adopt every part of the wider vision.

Are current family activities the same as the School beta?

Not necessarily. The homepage describes a School platform preparing for beta, while existing children and family activities have their own published pathway. Ask which offer is currently available, what stage it is at and how the two relate. Do not assume that a future platform feature is part of a present booking.

What if my child does not want to continue?

Raise that with the responsible team and listen to the child’s own account. Before starting, ask how pauses, withdrawal and guardian communication work for that activity. Participation should remain a choice rather than a test of the child’s openness, worth or willingness to meet an adult’s expectations.

A thoughtful beginning is enough

Good family learning questions make space for both practical clarity and the child’s experience. You can begin with an enquiry rather than a commitment. Read the current pathway, consider what your family needs to understand, and use the application and enquiry page to ask about the appropriate next step.
